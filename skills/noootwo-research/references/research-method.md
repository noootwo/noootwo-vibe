# Research Method

How to turn a blocked decision into evidence, and how to know when the evidence is enough.

## Question framing

Start from the decision, never the topic. Two questions decide the shape of the whole pass:

- What would change the answer? If nothing would, this is not research — it is a decision the user has already made.
- What would leave the answer unchanged? That is the floor: the minimum evidence below which more reading cannot help.

Write the queries to **separate candidates**, not to describe the field. "React state management" returns a landscape; "which of these two libraries survives a 10k-row table with optimistic updates" returns evidence.

## Query pattern

Combine these axes, three to six at a time:

- **Subject**: product type, component, library, stack, or workflow.
- **Task**: what the user is trying to do — monitor, compare, onboard, configure, create, migrate.
- **Platform**: web, React, Vue, Flutter, iOS, Android, desktop, server, edge.
- **Audience or scale**: consumer, pro tool, internal, enterprise; hundreds or millions of rows.
- **Constraint**: bundle budget, latency budget, offline, accessibility, licence, team skill.
- **Anti-pattern**: the thing being avoided — no card wall, no gradient SaaS, no unmaintained dependency, no lock-in.

## Breadth, then depth

1. **Fan out** — run the query set across independent source pools before reading anything closely. Breadth finds the shape of the answer.
2. **Triage** — keep the two or three candidates or mechanisms that survive contact with the constraints; drop the rest with a one-line reason.
3. **Go deep** — read those completely: the actual docs, the actual source, the actual benchmark, not a summary.
4. **Follow up** — one round of follow-up questions generated by what the deep read contradicted or left open. Cap it at one round; a second round means the question was framed wrong.

Stopping rule: stop when the next source would not flip the decision, or when two independent sources agree and the counterexample is understood.

## Evidence ladder

Rank what you find, and say which rung a claim sits on:

1. A running artifact, real product flow, repository, benchmark, or screenshot from the target stack.
2. Official documentation, platform guideline, or a public design system.
3. A credible hands-on report with visible output.
4. Community discussion with a visible artifact.
5. A single post, moodboard, portfolio shot, or trend essay.

Rungs 1–2 can decide. Rung 3 can decide when the artifact is visible. Rungs 4–5 suggest directions and never decide: they become a lead, and a lead needs a second independent source before it changes anything.

For a claim about what is *current*, *popular*, or *advanced*, record the date of the evidence. A high-signal source from three years ago answers a different question.

## Verification

- **Two independent sources** for any claim that decides something. Two links to the same announcement are one source.
- **Artifacts over prose**: a screenshot, repository, benchmark, or running example beats an article describing one.
- **Counterexample hunt**: find the case where the mechanism fails, or a well-regarded project that tried it and rejected it. A finding with no counterexample is usually under-researched, not universally true.
- **Generalisation check**: note the conditions under which the finding holds — scale, stack, team size, licence, region. Most bad research is a true finding applied outside its conditions.

## Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Collecting adjectives ("clean, modern, premium") | Nothing testable comes out; the decision is still open |
| Reading the first result deeply | Breadth is what finds the better candidate; depth first locks in the wrong one |
| One source deciding | It is a lead, and leads are how confident wrong answers start |
| Summarising a source instead of extracting a mechanism | The summary cannot be applied; the mechanism can |
| Copying a look, prompt, or brand surface | It is not a mechanism, and it carries licence and identity risk |
| Researching without a named decision | Produces notes nobody acts on, at full cost |
| Skipping the pass because the answer "seems obvious" | The obvious answer is the training-data answer, which is exactly what needs checking |

## Brief format

```markdown
Decision
- the choice, and what would have flipped it.

Finding
- the answer, in one paragraph.

Evidence
- source, evidence level, date, what it shows.

Mechanism
- what transfers, and why.

Boundary
- where it holds, where it does not, what not to copy.

Counterexample
- the case against it, or "none found".

Confidence
- high | medium | low, with the reason.

Open
- what would change this answer.
```
